## Bottom line (5 sentences max)

The strongest, best-evidenced structural edges for a sub-$100k solo operator are event-driven and
mechanical — closed-end fund discount convergence, odd-lot tender arbitrage, and SPAC trust-value
arbitrage — because they exploit institutional *inability* to hold small, illiquid, or
capital-inefficient positions, not a forecasting edge. The single biggest 2026 regime change for this
whole study is that FINRA's pattern-day-trader rule (the $25k / 3-trades-per-5-days constraint) was
eliminated effective June 4, 2026, replaced by real-time intraday margin monitoring — this removes
what was previously assumed to be the dominant structural constraint on a small account, though
broker rollout runs through October 2027 so behavior may lag the rule. Most "small-cap anomaly"
academic literature (January effect, small-cap premium, most factor edges below index-fund cutoffs)
shrinks or vanishes once realistic bid-ask spreads are applied, because the same illiquidity that
keeps institutions out also makes retail execution expensive. Prop-firm funded-account programs are,
on the balance of available evidence, predominantly fee-extraction businesses for the ~90-95% of
entrants who never get funded and the further attrition after that — legitimate for a very small
number of consistently profitable traders as cheap leverage, but not a reliable "route to capital."
The uncomfortable throughline: nearly every real edge here is compensation for illiquidity, manual
effort, or event risk that doesn't scale — not a free lunch, and several (CEF arb, odd-lot arb) are
openly "capacity-constrained" in the literature itself, meaning they stop working the moment enough
capital chases them, which is exactly why they still exist for a $1,000-$100,000 account.

## Candidate theses

### T6.1 — Closed-end fund (CEF) discount convergence is a real, exploitable, capacity-limited edge for accounts under ~$1-5M
- Evidence for: Academic backtests report large annualized "arbitrage returns" from buying deep-discount CEFs and selling premium ones: one study reports **18.2% annualized, Sharpe 1.918** for an expected-return-based long/short quintile strategy, and **14.9% annualized, Sharpe 1.519** for a simple current-discount quintile strategy, versus a market Sharpe of ~0.17 over the same period — both **[reported]**, single search-summarized academic source (CUNY presentation of a working paper), not independently re-derived. Returns reportedly are not explained by standard risk factors (i.e., not just beta in disguise). Discounts mechanically close via three real catalysts: fund liquidation/termination, open-ending (conversion to an ETF/mutual fund with NAV redemption), or activist-forced tender offers — all of which are documented, repeatable corporate events, not just "hope the discount narrows."
- Evidence against (including bid-ask spread arithmetic): CEFs are themselves less liquid than their underlying holdings — you're arbitraging one illiquid instrument against another. **[reported]** sources note the strategy has no built-in redemption mechanism (unlike ETFs), so unless a specific catalyst (liquidation/tender/activism) is present, "the discount could persist or widen indefinitely" — this is textbook value-trap risk, not risk-free arbitrage. Small/thinly-traded CEFs (the ones with the deepest discounts, which drive the backtested alpha) often have wide dollar spreads relative to their unit NAV (commonly cited informally as 1-3% round trip on the smallest funds); a naive long-only version of this strategy concentrated in the cheapest-discount decile is trading exactly the CEFs with the worst execution costs, which likely erodes a meaningful fraction of the ~15-18% headline number. No source found gives a net-of-realistic-spread backtest number — this is a real gap, flag it as **[unverified]** whether the edge survives.
- Why an institution can't or won't do this: A $1B fund buying a $50-200M CEF at a 10% discount either can't build a meaningful position without moving the price and blowing past ownership-disclosure/activism thresholds (13D triggers at 5% ownership, which then obligates costly activist campaigns to force the discount closed), or the position is too small relative to AUM to matter. The strategy's own literature is explicit that it's most reliable at CEF sizes ($10M-$500M) that are irrelevant to large funds.
- Is the return a free lunch, or compensation for illiquidity/tail-risk/effort? Compensation for illiquidity and patience risk (discount can widen for years before a catalyst), plus real effort to track catalysts (liquidation announcements, activist 13D filings, tender offers). Not a free lunch.
- Capital range where it works: Roughly $5,000-$1-2M. Below ~$5-10k, position sizing across enough CEFs to diversify catalyst risk (a single fund's liquidation timeline is binary/lumpy) becomes impractical given even small per-position commission/spread drag. Above a few million, you start moving the funds you're trying to buy.
- Kill test (runnable in under 1 day): Pull daily discount/premium history (CEFConnect or similar, free) for the current universe of US equity/fixed-income CEFs trading at >10% discount to NAV. Compute realized 6-month forward returns net of the actual bid-ask spread at time of entry (use quoted spread, not midpoint). If net-of-spread mean/median return on the deep-discount decile is not clearly positive and above the shallow-discount decile, the edge is spread-eaten and dead for a solo operator trading round lots at market.
- Verdict: **promising**, pending the spread-adjusted kill test — this is a real, documented, catalyst-driven mechanism, not a data-mined pattern, but the size of the *net* edge after realistic execution costs is not established from what I could verify.

### T6.2 — Odd-lot tender offers pay small holders a real, short-duration, near-risk-free premium that institutions cannot access
- Evidence for: Odd-lot tender provisions (buybacks that guarantee full, non-prorated repurchase for holders of <100 shares) exist specifically because odd-lot shareholders are disproportionately costly for issuers to administer (mailing, recordkeeping) — issuers pay a premium to clean them off the register. **[reported]** anecdotal/case examples cite an 18% return on capital over the announcement-to-completion window (~1 month) in one instance, and an 8.8% return in under 2 weeks in another; these appear to be single-event case studies from arbitrage newsletters/blogs, not a systematic academic backtest — treat the specific percentages as **[unverified]** representative, not typical.
- Evidence against (including bid-ask spread arithmetic): Because the tender price is fixed at announcement and the position is <100 shares, the position is tiny by construction (e.g., 99 shares × $30 = ~$3,000) — the dollar edge per event is small even if the percentage return is large, and building enough shares to hold a position while staying under the 100-share odd-lot cutoff requires care (buying up to 99 shares of a name pre-announcement is itself illiquid/spread-exposed if done speculatively before any tender is announced; buying *after* announcement to capture the guaranteed tender is the safer version but the market typically prices most of the spread away within hours of the announcement in liquid names). Spread arithmetic: if you're buying a $30 stock with a $0.05-0.10 spread post-announcement to lock in a tender at (say) a 10% premium, spread cost is ~0.2-0.3% against a 10% gain — spread is genuinely immaterial here *if* you can get filled at a reasonable size, which for a <100-share order is almost always true.
- Why an institution can't or won't do this: This is the cleanest "structural, not informational" edge in the whole set — the mechanism is legally defined around a share-count threshold institutions cannot go under (they hold thousands to millions of shares). A $1B fund literally cannot qualify for odd-lot preferential treatment. This is closer to true "free money" than anything else in this list.
- Is the return a free lunch, or compensation for illiquidity/tail-risk/effort? Mostly compensation for **effort and rarity**, not risk — you must find these offers (they are irregular, unadvertised beyond the tender filing, and require monitoring SEC EDGAR SC TO-I/SC 14D filings or a dedicated tracker), and the per-event dollar capture is small (a few hundred to low thousands of dollars), so the annualized return on a full-time-equivalent hour of monitoring effort is what's actually being tested, not return on capital.
- Capital range where it works: Effectively **any** capital, because position size per event is capped by the 99-share rule itself — this is a "several hundred to low thousand dollars per event, several events per year" strategy regardless of whether you have $1,000 or $100,000 to deploy. It doesn't scale up with capital; it scales with how many tenders you can find and act on.
- Kill test (runnable in under 1 day): Search SEC EDGAR full-text search for `SC TO-I` filings in the trailing 90 days containing "odd lot" provisions; compute, for each, the tender price vs. the pre-announcement closing price (the gross spread being offered) and whether the stock traded near the tender price within a day of announcement (if so, the market already closed the gap and there's no edge left to capture by the time a retail trader sees the news).
- Verdict: **promising but non-scaling** — real structural edge, but explicitly a manual, low-dollar-per-event, "operator effort" strategy, not a capital-allocation strategy. Good candidate for a small always-on scanner, bad candidate for meaningful account growth.

### T6.3 — SPAC pre-deal trust-value arbitrage is a real, low-risk, small-dollar carry trade — but current yields make it barely worth doing
- Evidence for: A SPAC's trust holds IPO proceeds in short-term Treasuries; NAV starts at $10.00 and accretes with interest; shareholders can redeem at NAV+accrued interest at the merger vote regardless of deal quality. **[reported]** an academic study ("A Sober Look at SPACs") found a mean annualized return of ~11.6% for the 2019-2020 SPAC cohort held to redemption, combining the risk-free trust yield plus the value of "free" warrants/rights received at IPO. This is a well-documented, structurally simple mechanism: buy at/near trust value, hold ~12-18 months to a deal vote, redeem for cash-plus-interest if the deal looks bad, or keep warrants if it looks good.
- Evidence against (including bid-ask spread arithmetic): The 11.6% figure is from the 2019-2020 SPAC boom cohort, a very different regime (near-zero rates made warrant optionality dominate the return; the SPAC boom also created scarcity value for shares near trust value). In 2026, short T-bill yields set the "risk-free" component floor lower relative to that era at the top of the cycle, and the SPAC market is a fraction of its 2021 size, meaning fewer opportunities and less warrant-value upside per deal to layer on top of the trust yield. SPAC common shares trading near trust value on small-cap SPACs can have wide dollar spreads on low daily volume — SPACs post-boom trade thin; a $0.05-0.10 spread on a $10-10.50 share is a 0.5-1% one-way cost, which is a meaningful bite out of a ~1-2%-over-T-bills incremental spread (the "trust arb premium" over just holding T-bills directly, which is the real comparison — you could just buy T-bills and skip the entire SPAC mechanism).
- Why an institution can't or won't do this: Large funds *do* participate in SPAC arb (Calamos and other named managers run dedicated SPAC-arb sleeves) — this is **not** exclusively a small-operator edge, contrary to this study's framing. Where the small-operator edge specifically survives is in very small SPAC deals ($50-150M trust size) where a fund's minimum position size would move the price, and in the tiny warrant/rights spreads on the smallest, least-followed SPACs that aren't worth an institutional analyst's time.
- Is the return a free lunch, or compensation for illiquidity/tail-risk/effort? Mostly a bond-like carry trade (compensation for locking capital ~12-18 months and monitoring redemption deadlines/proxy votes) plus a lottery-ticket component (warrant value if the eventual deal is good). Modest illiquidity premium on the smallest names.
- Capital range where it works: $1,000-$10M+ in principle (T-bill-like trade scales), but the actual *incremental edge over just buying T-bills* is thin enough today that below a few thousand dollars the effort of tracking redemption deadlines and proxy mechanics isn't worth it versus buying a T-bill ETF directly.
- Kill test (runnable in under 1 day): Pull the current universe of pre-deal SPACs trading below the trust NAV+accrued-interest (screen via a SPAC tracker site), and compute the annualized yield-to-redemption at current market price versus the current 3-month T-bill yield. If the SPAC arb annualized yield is not clearly above the T-bill rate after the spread cost of entry, there is no edge over just holding T-bills — this is the whole test.
- Verdict: **marginal** in the current (2026) regime specifically because it's a rates-relative trade and rates/opportunity set have both compressed since the studied cohort; it is not exclusively small-operator territory the way T6.2 is.

### T6.4 — Small deal merger arbitrage carries a real but thin, size-dependent spread that shrinks the more capital chases it
- Evidence for: **[reported]** deals with 5-7.5% spreads returned ~4.0% on average; 0-2.5%-spread deals returned only ~0.9%; blended average across all deals (including busted ones) is ~1.5% on long-only exposure. Small/micro-cap deals reportedly get regulatory approval more easily than large-cap (less antitrust scrutiny) even though liquidity/size constraints otherwise work against them — this is the one place small deals structurally have an *edge* over large ones (faster, more certain closes), not just lower institutional interest.
- Evidence against (including bid-ask spread arithmetic): Merger arb spreads have compressed >400bps industry-wide since 2002 as more capital (dedicated hedge funds) has chased the strategy — the "small deal funds ignore" framing needs a caveat: dedicated merger-arb funds still monitor essentially all announced US deals regardless of size, because deal flow, not capital, is the scarce resource for them; a $500M-$5B deal fund happily takes a $50M small-cap deal if the annualized spread is attractive. The "small deals are neglected" thesis is weaker than commonly assumed for *announced, cash* deals. Spread arithmetic: on a typical small-cap cash deal trading at, say, $9.80 against a $10.00 deal price with a $0.05 bid-ask spread, the spread cost alone is ~0.5% of the 2% gross spread — a quarter of the edge, before commissions (near zero) — meaningful but not fatal on a well-priced deal; it becomes fatal on thin small-cap names with $0.20+ spreads relative to a $0.20 deal spread (i.e., spread cost equals or exceeds the entire arbitrage profit).
- Why an institution can't or won't do this: The genuine small-operator edge is in *stock-for-stock* small deals requiring a short leg (shorting the acquirer to hedge the exchange ratio) where the target's borrow may be tight or the position too small to be worth a fund's operational overhead, and in truly micro deals (<$25-50M) below the minimum deal size most professional arb funds will underwrite given their own fixed research/legal-review cost per deal.
- Is the return a free lunch, or compensation for illiquidity/tail-risk/effort? Compensation for deal-break tail risk (you lose ~2.8% on average on a busted deal per the cited study, versus gaining ~2% on a completed one — the strategy is a risk premium for bearing regulatory/financing/MAC-clause risk), not a free lunch.
- Capital range where it works: $5,000-$50M roughly; below ~$5k, diversifying across enough simultaneous deals to avoid single-deal-break concentration risk is hard; the "neglected small deal" edge specifically is largest in the $10-100M deal-size range.
- Kill test (runnable in under 1 day): Pull the current list of pending US cash M&A deals under $200M deal value (13D/DEFM14A filings or a free merger tracker), compute annualized spread at last close net of the quoted bid-ask spread, and compare against the 4.0%/0.9% benchmarks above — if net spreads on the smallest deals aren't higher than net spreads on comparable larger deals, the "small deal edge" claim fails immediately.
- Verdict: **marginal** — real but thinner than commonly claimed, and the "institutions ignore small deals" premise is only partly true (they ignore small deals mainly when spread-per-unit-of-research-cost is unattractive, not because of size per se).

### T6.5 — Index reconstitution price pressure (Russell 2000/S&P 600 additions and deletions) is real and documented but is now heavily front-run
- Evidence for: **[reported]** deleted stocks show negative abnormal returns around deletion with partial-to-mostly-permanent effects (estimates range widely across studies, roughly half to three-quarters of the initial price move persists per one source), and one source found deleted Russell 2000 names fell ~7.6% around reconstitution before rebounding ~16.1% over the following 12 months — i.e., a documented reversal that a patient buyer of forced-sale names can harvest. Added names show a positive, partially-permanent price bump from the same forced-buying mechanism in reverse.
- Evidence against (including bid-ask spread arithmetic): This is one of the most heavily studied and heavily traded anomalies in market microstructure — HBS's own "Disappearing Index Effect" working paper (found in the same search) is explicitly about how this effect has shrunk over time as more capital anticipates and front-runs the reconstitution date. The stocks being deleted are, by definition, small, falling, illiquid names — exactly the ones with the worst spreads. A stock being kicked out of the Russell 2000 for falling below the cutoff is often a $50-300M market cap name with real spread costs of 0.3-1%+ round trip, which eats directly into a reversal trade sized around a known, calendar-fixed event that thousands of quant funds already trade.
- Why an institution can't or won't do this: Ironically, large systematic funds are the primary traders of this exact effect (reconstitution-arbitrage is a well-known strategy at multi-strategy funds) — this is **not** a small-operator-exclusive edge; if anything a small operator is late to a crowded trade. Where a genuine small-operator edge might remain is in the smallest, most illiquid names at the very bottom of the Russell 2000/microcap 2000 cutoff where a fund's minimum trade size or compliance/reporting overhead isn't worth the effort for a single name — but this is a thin, name-specific edge, not the systematic reconstitution effect itself.
- Is the return a free lunch, or compensation for illiquidity/tail-risk/effort? Compensation for illiquidity (you're buying names everyone else just sold) and for being willing to hold a distressed name through further potential decline before any reversal shows up — real tail risk, not a free lunch, and it's crowded.
- Capital range where it works: Not clearly favorable at any capital range given the crowding — this looks more like a "dead/crowded" thesis for solo alpha, though it may still work as a rules-based tax-loss-harvesting-timing overlay (see T6.6) rather than a standalone alpha source.
- Kill test (runnable in under 1 day): Backtest a simple rule (buy Russell 2000 deletions on the reconstitution effective date, hold 12 months, net of a 0.5% round-trip cost assumption) against just holding the Russell 2000 index over the same window, using the last 3-5 reconstitution dates (last Friday of June). If it doesn't beat the index net of that cost assumption, treat as dead.
- Verdict: **marginal-to-dead** as a standalone thesis — real, well-documented, but heavily arbitraged by exactly the institutions it's claimed to exclude.

### T6.6 — December tax-loss-selling pressure on small, illiquid, heavily-down names creates a real but narrow and thinning January reversal
- Evidence for: **[reported]** the January effect (small-cap outperformance concentrated in early January) is one of the most replicated anomalies in finance (Keim/Reinganum, 1983 onward); tax-loss-selling is the leading explanation — concentrated December selling in losers depresses prices into year-end, and the selling pressure's absence in January lets prices revert.
- Evidence against (including bid-ask spread arithmetic): Multiple sources agree the effect has "considerably diminished since the 1990s" and is now concentrated narrowly in "small, illiquid, heavily sold names" rather than small caps broadly — i.e., the exact same illiquid corner where spreads are worst. A name that fell 40-60% during the year (the kind that generates real tax-loss selling) is very likely to also carry a 2-5%+ round-trip spread by December, which can consume a meaningful fraction of a "few percent" January reversal.
- Why an institution can't or won't do this: Funds with fiscal years, benchmark tracking, and window-dressing incentives are often *part of the December selling pressure* (window dressing: funds sell losers before year-end reports), not exempt from it — so this is more "structural forced-seller mechanic that a small buyer can lean against" than "institutions structurally excluded." A small operator's genuine edge is being willing to buy the ugliest, smallest, most-beaten-down names in December that an institution's mandate (min market cap, min price, "no distressed names" policies) forbids it from holding even temporarily.
- Is the return a free lunch, or compensation for illiquidity/tail-risk/effort? Compensation for illiquidity and holding names actively being dumped for non-fundamental reasons — real risk that "beaten down" sometimes means "beaten down for a fundamental reason," not just tax-driven.
- Capital range where it works: $1,000-$500k; concentrated, small-position-count strategy given the narrow universe of qualifying names each December.
- Kill test (runnable in under 1 day): Screen for stocks down >40% YTD with market cap <$300M as of mid-December in a recent year (use 2-3 historical Decembers), and compute January (first 10-15 trading days) return net of an assumed 1.5-3% round-trip spread cost, versus the small-cap index over the same window.
- Verdict: **marginal** — plausible mechanism, narrow and shrinking universe, and the spread cost genuinely threatens to eat most of the edge; worth the 1-day kill test before committing more research time.

## Broker/API landscape for a solo dev in 2026

- **Alpaca**: **[reported]** Commission-free US equities/ETFs, fractional shares down to $1 notional, well-documented REST/WebSocket API built for algo trading specifically, no account minimum for the core brokerage account. Crypto trading carries 0.15-0.25% fees. Commission-free status can be affected when accessed through certain business-partner integrations rather than directly. Widely considered the default choice for a solo Python-first quant developer.
- **Interactive Brokers (IBKR)**: **[reported]** IBKR Lite = commission-free US stocks/ETFs; IBKR Pro = tiered commissions with better execution/routing control. Options ~$0.65/contract. Broadest asset-class coverage (equities, options, futures, forex, bonds, international) of any retail-accessible API — relevant if a thesis (e.g., short-side small-cap, SPAC warrants, or margin/short mechanics) needs instruments Alpaca doesn't support. API has real rate limits (50 order msgs/sec, 100 concurrent market-data lines cited) that matter for a scanner hitting many small-cap/CEF names.
- **Tradier**: **[reported]** Subscription-style pricing, commission-free options with small per-contract fee, API rate limits 120 req/min standard or 600 req/min on a premium tier, positioned specifically for options-systematic traders.
- **Schwab**: **[reported]** Free stock/ETF trading (post-TD Ameritrade API migration); specific 2026 algo-API fee detail was not found in search results — verify directly before relying on it (WebFetch to schwab.com is blocked in this environment).
- General 2026 note: the "minimum commission eats a small position" problem that historically hurt small accounts on plain equities has **largely disappeared** among these brokers; it persists for options and for any thesis requiring instruments (OTC pink sheets, certain CEFs, SPAC warrants) that not every broker/API supports cleanly — confirm instrument-level API support before designing a strategy around T6.1/T6.3.

## Prop-firm funded accounts: honest assessment

- **[reported]** Challenge-to-funded pass rates cluster around **5-14%** across the sources found (FTMO cited around 9-10% for its standard 2-step challenge in one source; other estimates for first-attempt pass rates range as high as 15-25%, but these higher numbers are less consistently sourced and may reflect self-reported firm marketing rather than independent audits — treat the 5-14% band as more credible).
- **[reported]** The funnel narrows sharply after passing: one source states only ~7% of *all challenge buyers* ever receive a payout, and that of traders who do pass (5-10%), only ~20% of *those* go on to receive an actual payout — i.e., **on the order of 1-2% of people who buy a challenge ever see a payment**, per one source's own framing. These figures are self-reported by firms or trade-press aggregators, not independently audited — flag as **[unverified]** in precision, though directionally consistent across multiple sources.
- **[reported]** Payout terms at a leading firm (FTMO): 80-90% profit split depending on account tier; challenge fee reportedly refunded on first payout; withdrawal turnaround commonly cited as 24-48 hours after the 14-day-from-first-trade eligibility window.
- **Regulatory reality check**: **[reported]** The CFTC's 2023 fraud action against My Forex Funds (then the largest forex prop firm globally) alleged the firm was not actually funding traders but running a simulated-trading platform that profited from trader losses, manipulated execution to fail evaluations, and concealed ~$310M in client funds. Notably, this specific case was later dismissed with prejudice in 2025 after a Special Master found the CFTC had misled the court and sanctioned the CFTC $3.1M in costs — **the dismissal was about CFTC procedural/evidentiary conduct, not a finding that the underlying business-model allegations were false.** Read as: the prop-firm-as-fee-extraction-business concern is credible enough that a federal regulator brought the case, but the one major test case collapsed on the regulator's own errors rather than resolving the underlying question.
- **Verdict**: The evidence supports treating funded-account programs as **predominantly a fee-extraction business at the population level** (single-digit-percent ultimate payout rates, revenue model built on challenge-fee churn from a large base of people who fail), while being a **legitimate, cheap source of leverage for the small minority of traders who are already consistently profitable on their own capital** and treat the challenge fee as a call option on getting funded rather than as an investment expected to pay off on its own. For this project's specific context (an operator seeking a *verified* edge with real capital, not a marketing/leverage play), a funded-account program is not a substitute for proving an edge — it is, at best, a distribution channel to size up an edge that is already independently verified profitable, and should be evaluated only after T6.1-T6.6-style edges (or others from this research effort) are proven, not before.
